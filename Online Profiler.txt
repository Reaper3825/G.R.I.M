A — How profile-correlation / doxxing typically works (high level)

Attackers stitch together pieces of public information to link an online handle to a real identity. They don’t usually have a magic trick — they build a chain of small connections that, together, become convincing. Common building blocks include:

• Username reuse across sites. People use the same or similar nicknames on Xbox, Steam, Discord, Twitter, Reddit, etc. Matching a unique username across sites quickly produces accounts to inspect.
• Shared profile images / avatars. The same profile photo used in multiple places is a strong link. Reverse image searching an avatar often reveals other places it’s used (social media, forum posts, art sites).
• Public social posts and context clues. Posts that casually reveal location, school, workplace, local events, or photo backgrounds (landmarks, license plates) can be correlated.
• Linked accounts and OAuth leaks. Accounts that show “connected social” or link to a Twitch/YouTube/Discord can expose more identities.
• Friends & mutuals. Public friend lists, follower/following graphs, and screenshots can reveal personal connections who themselves have public info.
• Metadata (images/files). Photos sometimes include EXIF metadata (camera model, GPS tags, timestamps). Many leak location if not stripped.
• Data brokers & breaches. Publicly leaked records, paste sites, and commercial people-search services can provide name/email/phone that, when combined with other data, tie back to you.
• WHOIS / domain / email traces. If you ever used a domain/email tied to your name, that can be found and correlated.
• Operational mistakes. Posting a screenshot that includes your desktop clock, local weather, or a partially visible receipt, or joining voice chat with a linked Discord account, gives clues an investigator can use.

Again: these are categories of signals — I’m not providing instructions for collecting them. The point is: doxxers rely on many small, plausible links, not a single perfect one.

B — Prioritized defensive checklist (do these now, in order)

I’ll give specific, practical actions you can perform to break the chains above.

1) Account & authentication hygiene (highest priority)

Enable 2-factor authentication (authenticator app preferred) on your Microsoft/Xbox account and on every major account (email, Discord, Steam, Twitch, social).

Use a password manager and unique passwords for every service. Change any reused password immediately.

Lock your email and phone: set a carrier PIN to reduce SIM-swap risk.

2) Audit usernames, avatars, and linked accounts

Make a short list of all places you used the same/similar username. For each: either change the username to something unique-per-platform or make the account private.

Replace matching avatars with platform-specific images (don’t reuse the same face/photo). Avoid personal photos as avatars.

Remove or unlink connected accounts (Twitter, YouTube, Discord, Twitch) from your Xbox profile or other public profiles unless necessary.

3) Lock down privacy settings

Xbox: Settings → Account → Privacy & online safety — set messaging/see-profile/invite settings to Friends or Friends-of-Friends, not Everyone. Hide real name and location.

Social media: set profiles private where possible, remove public phone/email fields, and remove location from posts/bios.

Review OAuth app permissions on your email and social accounts; revoke anything unfamiliar.

4) Clean up existing public content

Delete or unlist old posts, images, forum threads, Twitch/VODs, or bios that mention your real name, hometown, school, workplace, phone, or email.

Replace personal profile photos with generic, non-identifying images (no real face or unique objects).

Consider changing usernames on high-indexing sites (Reddit, Twitter, Steam) if they link to your identity.

5) Images & metadata

Strip EXIF metadata from all photos before posting. Use your phone/editor or a tool (e.g., in general terms: photo app settings or an EXIF-stripper) to remove GPS/timestamps.

Avoid posting photos that show unique interior details, street signs, receipts, or license plates.

6) Separate identity & payment signals

Use a separate email for gaming profiles that is not tied to your real name or job. Use disposable or alias emails for public registrations.

If you stream or accept tips, use payment channels that don’t expose your real name or home address (set up business or PO box handling where needed).

7) Network & device-level defenses

If you’re being targeted with network attacks, power-cycle your modem/router to get a new dynamic IP (many ISPs assign dynamic IPs). Contact your ISP if attacks persist.

Consider a router-level VPN if you want to hide your home IP from accidental exposure — note this may complicate console NAT and online play.

Keep consoles/PCs fully patched and avoid running unknown payloads or VPNs that promise “anti-DDoS” without trust.

8) Monitor & detection

Add your email to breach notification services (e.g., HaveIBeenPwned) and enable alerts.

Regularly search for your username and your real name to see what’s publicly indexed; remove or request takedowns when possible. (This is for defense — you can do private checks on yourself.)

9) Prepare an incident pack

Keep a folder with screenshots, timestamps (UTC), chat logs, match/game session IDs, and any messages. Don’t edit originals.

Know how to report on each platform: Xbox Support, Discord Trust & Safety, Steam, platform publisher abuse forms. Include the preserved evidence.

10) Legal & escalation

For real threats, harassment, or doxxing, report to platform support and local law enforcement. The police can subpoena platform logs (they’re the only ones who can reliably map an IP to a subscriber).

If someone publishes your address/phone, request takedowns from the host and from search engines (Google has remove URL options). Keep records of takedown requests.

Use a burner email as your Xbox account recovery address if you want separation from your personal email.

Short answer: Sherlock is an open-source OSINT tool that searches for a given username across a large number of websites to find accounts that match that handle. It's commonly used for defensive self-audits (to see where your username appears) and by investigators for non-malicious research — but only run it against usernames you own or have permission to probe.

What it does

Takes a username (or list of usernames) and checks many popular services (social networks, forums, code hosts, image sites, etc.) for that exact handle.

Returns URLs where the username is found (or likely found), which lets you quickly see where an attacker could correlate your gamertag to other profiles.

Optionally produces CSV/JSON output for later review or automation.

Legality & ethics

Legal to use for defensive self-audits on your own handles.

Do not use it to probe other people without permission — that can be intrusive and violate terms of service.

Treat results as leads, not proof: matching usernames aren’t definitive identity links (but they’re useful signals for what to remediate).

Quick install & run (typical)

(These commands are the usual pattern — run on your own machine.)

# clone the repo
git clone https://github.com/sherlock-project/sherlock.git
cd sherlock

# create venv (recommended)
python3 -m venv .venv
source .venv/bin/activate    # or .\.venv\Scripts\activate on Windows

# install deps
python3 -m pip install -r requirements.txt

# run a single username
python3 sherlock.py yourusername

# run and save results as JSON
python3 sherlock.py yourusername --outputfile results.json --format json


(Repository name and exact commands are stable but may change slightly across versions; above is the standard workflow.)

Useful options

Run against a file of usernames to sweep multiple handles.

Export results to CSV/JSON for importing into your personal_osint_profile.md.

Configure threads or timeouts to tune speed vs server load.
